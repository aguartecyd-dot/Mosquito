About the research

Mosquitoes spread dengue, Zika and malaria[cite: 2]. Because vaccines and treatments are limited for some of these diseases, controlling the vector is a main line of defence[cite: 2]. Insecticides work, but resistance builds up and non-target species suffer[cite: 2].

The Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) takes a different route[cite: 2]. Male mosquitoes are reared in large numbers and sterilised, usually by radiation[cite: 2]. They are released into the wild population, where they compete for mates[cite: 2]. A wild female that mates with a sterile male lays no viable eggs[cite: 2]. Each release therefore cuts the next generation, and repeated releases can drive the population down[cite: 2].

Males do not bite, so released sterile males add no disease risk[cite: 2]. SIT is also species-specific: it only affects the species that was released[cite: 2, 3].

Field trials are slow and costly. This simulator lets researchers test release sizes and intervals on a virtual neighbourhood before committing resources[cite: 3, 4, 11]. It is a simplified teaching and research model, not a forecast for any real location[cite: 1, 4].
